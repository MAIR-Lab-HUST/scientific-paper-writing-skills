# Argument Design

Use this reference when planning a study, identifying the paper's central contribution, or repairing a narrative that is more confident than its evidence.

## Build a claim spine

Write the smallest complete chain before drafting prose:

| Link | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Problem | What specific limitation, uncertainty, or need matters? |
| Testable claim | What observable proposition could be wrong? |
| Method | What design can distinguish the claim from nearby alternatives? |
| Evidence | Which result bears on the proposition, under which conditions? |
| Scope | Where does the claim apply, and where is it untested? |

Each link should name its conditions. A broad motivation does not itself support a broad conclusion.

## Keep statement types separate

| Type | Permitted content | Check before use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | Measured, calculated, or directly reported result | Can a reader locate the result and its conditions? |
| Hypothesis | Proposed mechanism, prediction, or explanation | What observation would count against it? |
| Supported explanation | Interpretation consistent with specified results and tests | Which alternative explanations were weakened, and which remain? |

Use verbs that match the status: report or observe for observations; predict or propose for hypotheses; support, suggest, or are consistent with for explanations. Avoid presenting an explanation as a fact merely because it is plausible.

## Calibrate claim strength

Classify the intended claim before choosing wording:

- **Descriptive:** a pattern was observed in a defined setting.
- **Comparative:** one condition differs from another under a matched protocol.
- **Explanatory:** a mechanism is supported after relevant rivals are addressed.
- **Generalizing:** a pattern extends beyond the tested setting.

Move upward only when the evidence supports the stronger class. If a result is limited to a dataset, instrument, population, or operating range, carry that boundary into the claim.

## Stress-test the narrative

For each headline claim, ask:

1. What competing cause, confound, selection effect, or measurement artifact could produce the same pattern?
2. Which existing result bears on that alternative?
3. What minimal new test would produce different outcomes under the competing accounts?
4. What result would require narrowing, revising, or abandoning the claim?

When a discriminator is unavailable, say that the evidence is compatible with multiple explanations. This is often more useful than adding speculative detail.

## Useful outputs

Choose a compact format suited to the request: a five-link claim spine, a ranked list of claims and gaps, a story card, or an outline whose headings state the evidence role of each section.
